How to size it

Do not multiply a running-watt range by 24 hours. That would invent a duty cycle. Honda lists an Energy Star refrigerator/freezer at 132–192 W running and 1,200 W to start. Champion lists a refrigerator at 150–400 W running. Those numbers tell you the machine is on or starting. They do not tell you how many hours a day the compressor runs. Battle Born's battery article is the source that actually states a daily example: 100 W × 5 h = 500 Wh. Keep that label on it — an example — and replace it with a plug-in meter.

  1. Example energy: 100 × 5 = 500 Wh.
  2. Array at 4 peak sun hours: 500 ÷ 4 = 125 W. Plus 20% = 150 W. Plus 30% = 162.5 W, about 163 W.
  3. At 5 peak sun hours: 500 ÷ 5 = 100 W, then 120 W and 130 W.
  4. Phoenix December check: 500 ÷ 4.75 = 105.3 W, then 126.3 W and 136.8 W. The annual plane-of-array value in that run is 6.54; December is the 4.75 figure.
  5. Illustrative 400 W panels: 162.5 ÷ 400 rounds up to 1 panel. A 200 W kit search is the shopping step for this small array; the count above is still just a divisor.

500 Wh ÷ 12 V = 41.7 Ah for the example day, and 83.3 Ah if you double for two days. The 12 V figure is the one in 2,400 Wh ÷ 12 V = 200 Ah. Size that bank on the battery bank sizer.

There is a second solar number that is easy to mix up with 125 W. Battle Born's panel article also says about 200 W of solar per 100 Ah of LiFePO4. The 500 Wh example at 12 V is 41.7 Ah, under 100 Ah, so that guideline is answering "how much array per battery," which is the panels-to-charge-a-battery page. It is not a revision of 500 ÷ 4.

Meter the fridge, swap 500 Wh for the measured day, and repeat the division. The solar size calculator and PVWatts are the place to do that with your sun, not Phoenix's.

How many watts does a refrigerator use?

Updated October 3, 2026. Running watts and daily watt-hours are different measurements. Honda lists an Energy Star refrigerator/freezer at 132–192 W running and 1,200 W to start. Champion lists a refrigerator at 150–400 W running. Those are the watt figures on the charts. The only daily energy this page uses is still Battle Born's example, 100 W × 5 hours = 500 Wh.

That 500 Wh in this site's calculator

The solar size calculator runs sizeSystem in assets/app.js. The array line is daily watt-hours ÷ sun hours ÷ 0.75, rounded up to the next 100 W. The comment above that function describes 0.75 as wiring, controller, battery round-trip, and panel heat and soiling. The bank line is daily watt-hours × autonomy days ÷ max depth of discharge, and amp-hours round up to the next 10 Ah. The charge-controller function divides array watts by battery volts by 0.85 and takes the first of 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, and 100 A that is at least that current.

Inputs: 500 Wh, sun hours 4, autonomy 2 days, max depth of discharge 80% (the LiFePO4 option), battery voltage 12 V. The form opens on 24 V. The 12 V run is the one that lines up with Battle Born's 12 V example.

  • Array: 500 ÷ 4 ÷ 0.75 = 166.67 W, which rounds up to 200 W.
  • Bank: 500 × 2 ÷ 0.8 = 1,250 Wh. 1,250 ÷ 12 = 104.17 Ah, which rounds up to 110 Ah.
  • Winter line in the same function, using its 2.5 sun hours: 500 ÷ 2.5 ÷ 0.75 = 266.67 W, which rounds up to 300 W.
  • Controller: 200 ÷ 12 ÷ 0.85 = 19.6 A, so the listed size is 20 A.

Leave voltage on the 24 V default and the array stays 200 W. The bank becomes 1,250 ÷ 24 = 52.08 Ah, which rounds up to 60 Ah. Controller current becomes 200 ÷ 24 ÷ 0.85 = 9.8 A, so 10 A.

Battle Born's battery article does not divide by 0.8. It divides watt-hours by system voltage: 500 ÷ 12 = 41.7 Ah for one day, and two days doubles that to 83.3 Ah. The BB10012i manual rates that model at 100% usable depth of discharge, which is why their two-day figure still fits in one 100 Ah battery. The calculator's 80% option is a separate input, and it is the input that produces 110 Ah. Counting those amp-hours as batteries is on how many solar batteries do I need.

A common mistake with the watt number

Typing Champion's 150 W as if the compressor ran 24 hours hands the calculator 3,600 Wh. Then 3,600 ÷ 4 ÷ 0.75 = 1,200 W exactly, so the array line prints 1,200 W. That is the function obeying the hours you typed. Honda and Champion publish running watts and, on Honda's chart, starting watts. They do not publish those hours. The 1,200 W starting figure from Honda is a generator starting load, not an array size.

Limits

Meter the fridge and replace 500 Wh. A licensed electrician handles the branch circuit the refrigerator plugs into. This calculator does not size that circuit. What a published degradation rate does to the 200 W round-up is on solar panel degradation.

FAQ

Where does 500 Wh come from?

Battle Born's battery article uses a refrigerator example of 100 W × 5 hours = 500 Wh. It is their example. It is not a measurement of your fridge.

What array does that 500 Wh need?

500 ÷ 4 peak sun hours = 125 W. With 20 to 30 percent more, that is 150–162.5 W, and 162.5 W rounds to about 163 W.

Can I size from the 150–400 W running range?

Champion lists a refrigerator at 150–400 W running, and Honda lists an Energy Star refrigerator/freezer at 132–192 W running (1,200 W to start). Those are running or starting watts. Turning them into watt-hours needs hours of compressor run time, which these sources do not give. Use a plug-in meter.

What does 200 W per 100 Ah change?

Battle Born recommends about 200 W of solar per 100 Ah of LiFePO4. That sizes the array to the battery. The 125 W on this page sizes the array to the 500 Wh example. Both can be true; they are different questions.

What does this site's calculator return for the 500 Wh example?

At 4 sun hours, 2 days of autonomy, 80% depth of discharge, and 12 V: 500 ÷ 4 ÷ 0.75 = 166.67 W, which rounds up to 200 W. The bank is 500 × 2 ÷ 0.8 = 1,250 Wh, and 1,250 ÷ 12 rounds up to 110 Ah. Controller current is 200 ÷ 12 ÷ 0.85 = 19.6 A, so 20 A.
